Part One

What is Development?

Development is much more than making money. It's about improving every aspect of human life — health, education, freedom, equality, and dignity.

1.1 Core Definitions — What the Scholars Say

Three major scholars define development in slightly different ways. You need to know all three, but especially Sen.

Goulet (1971)

Development = Life Sustenance (basic survival needs) + Self-Esteem (dignity, respect) + Freedom (ability to choose your own path)

Todaro & Smith (2020)

Development = simultaneous improvement across economic, social, political, and environmental dimensions — all at once, not just income growth.

⭐ Amartya Sen (1999) — MOST IMPORTANT

Development = expanding people's CAPABILITIES — what people are able to do and be. It's not about how much money you earn, but whether you can live a long, healthy, dignified, productive life. Sen argues you need opportunities, resources, and freedoms to achieve this.

💡
Exam Tip

In any question about development, Sen is the first scholar to cite. His magic word is "capabilities" — what people are able to do, not just what they earn. Write it exactly: "expanding human capabilities and freedoms."

1.2 Traditional vs. Modern View of Development

AspectTraditional (Old) ViewModern View
Main FocusGDP growth, industrialization, tradeQuality of life, human well-being, freedom
Core BeliefTrickle-Down: income growth benefits everyone eventuallyHolistic: economic + social + cultural + political + ecological
Why are people poor?Insufficient capital or market restrictionsLack of freedoms, exclusion, structural inequality
How to measure?GDP onlyGDP + literacy + health + gender equality + environment
⚠️
The Trickle-Down Myth

The Trickle-Down Hypothesis says: make rich people richer and the wealth will "trickle down" to the poor. Modern economics disproves this. A country's GDP can grow while inequality rises — the poor get nothing. This is the key failure of the traditional view.

1.3 Economic Growth vs. Economic Development

These two terms seem similar but mean very different things:

Economic Growth
  • Just an increase in the VALUE of goods/services produced (GDP)
  • QUANTITATIVE — we count money and output
  • Can happen WITH rising inequality
  • Example: More factory output, even if workers stay poor
Economic Development
  • Improvement in well-being, social progress, freedom, equality
  • QUALITATIVE — how people actually live their lives
  • Requires equity and inclusion — benefits must reach everyone
  • Example: Better schools, hospitals, governance, rights
The Key Difference in One Sentence

A country can grow GDP by expanding coal production — but if this damages health, destroys the environment, and increases inequality, that is growth WITHOUT development. Growth is just numbers. Development is about people's lives.

Part Two

Economic Growth — Formulas Explained

Every formula explained in plain English. For exams: state the formula → define each variable → explain what it measures → give an example.

2.1 Nominal GDP

What it is in plain English: The total market value of everything a country produces in a year, measured at today's prices. The problem? If prices go up (inflation), Nominal GDP goes up even if you made the exact same amount of stuff.

Formula
Nominal GDP = Σ (Pit × Qit)
Pit = Price of good/service i in the CURRENT year (today's price)
Qit = Quantity of good/service i produced in year t
Σ = "Sum of" — add up all goods and services

2.2 Real GDP

What it is in plain English: GDP adjusted for inflation. We use prices from a fixed "base year" so we can compare actual production over time — not just price changes. Real GDP answers: "How much did we actually produce?"

Two Ways to Calculate
Option 1: Real GDP = Σ (Pi0 × Qit)
Pi0 = BASE YEAR price (fixed, not today's price — this removes inflation)
Qit = Quantity produced in year t (what we actually made)

Option 2: Real GDP = (Nominal GDP / GDP Deflator) × 100
GDP Deflator = A price index that measures overall inflation across the economy

2.3 Per Capita GDP

What it is in plain English: The average income per person. Divide total GDP by the total population to get a sense of individual living standards.

Formula
Per Capita GDP = GDP ÷ Population
If GDP is nominal → result is Nominal Per Capita GDP
If GDP is real → result is Real Per Capita GDP (more accurate for comparisons)

2.4 Economic Growth Rate

What it is in plain English: How fast is the economy growing? This formula calculates the percentage change in Real GDP between two years.

Formula
g = ((Yt − Yt-1) / Yt-1) × 100
Yt = Real GDP in the CURRENT year
Yt-1 = Real GDP in the PREVIOUS year
g = Growth rate expressed as a percentage

What drives economic growth? Capital accumulation (factories, machines, skills), technological progress, labour force growth, and good institutions and policies.

Part Three

Theories of Development

Each theory answers one question: WHY do some countries develop while others stay poor? Know the scholar, the core idea, the key terms, and always link to Sri Lanka.

T1

Classical Economic Theory

Adam Smith, Ricardo, Mill
Core Argument Free markets are the best way to allocate resources and drive development. If you let markets work without government interference, growth happens naturally.
Invisible Hand (Adam Smith)

People acting in their own self-interest unknowingly benefit society. A baker makes bread to earn money — but bread also feeds the community. No one planned it; the "invisible hand" of the market coordinates everything.

Laissez-Faire

French for "leave alone." The government should not interfere with markets. Let supply and demand work freely — the market will find the right price and the right amount to produce.

Division of Labour

Breaking production into specialised tasks massively increases efficiency. Smith's famous example: in a pin factory, one person doing all steps makes 1 pin/day. But 10 people each doing one step make thousands/day.

Trickle-Down Hypothesis

If national income grows, benefits will eventually reach all citizens — wealth "trickles down" from rich to poor. Note: Modern economics shows this doesn't automatically happen — it is now considered outdated thinking.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Post-1977 economic liberalisation (open economy, free trade, privatisation) followed Classical principles. Export Processing Zones (EPZs) like Katunayake attracted Foreign Direct Investment. But it widened inequality between regions — showing Classical theory's weakness.

T2

Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth

Walt Rostow, 1960
Core Argument ALL countries follow the SAME five-stage path from traditional farming society to modern consumer economy. Development is like a staircase — every country must climb the same steps.
S1
Traditional Society

Economy dominated by subsistence farming (people grow food just to survive). Low technology, wealth concentrated among landowners. Almost no industry.

S2
Preconditions for Take-off

Country starts building the foundation for industry: roads, banks, savings begin, an entrepreneurial class emerges, and a central state forms.

S3
Take-off ← THE KEY STAGE

20–30 years of rapid, self-sustaining growth. Investment rises sharply from ~5% to over 10% of national income. One or two leading industries (textiles, railways) drive growth. The economy becomes self-propelling.

S4
Drive to Maturity

Long period of sustained growth. Economy diversifies, technology spreads to all sectors, workers become skilled, country integrates into global trade.

S5
High Mass Consumption

The ultimate goal. Economy shifts to consumer goods and services. Per capita income rises significantly. Focus moves to social welfare and quality of life.

Criticisms

  • Eurocentric and Linear: Assumes all countries follow the Western (UK/USA) path — ignores colonial history and cultural differences
  • Vague Take-off: What exactly triggers the Take-off stage? Hard to identify in practice
  • Ignores colonialism: Doesn't explain how colonial history blocked development for many countries
  • Internal inconsistencies: Canada and Australia reached Mass Consumption before full industrial maturity
🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Colonial era = Traditional Stage. Post-independence reforms = Preconditions. 1977 open economy = attempted Take-off. But Sri Lanka has NOT fully reached Drive to Maturity — limited innovation, policy instability, and foreign exchange crises hold it back.

T3

Structural Theory

Prebisch & Singer, 1950s
Core Argument The global trading system is UNFAIR and structurally keeps poor countries poor. Developing countries export cheap raw materials while developed countries sell expensive manufactured goods — and this gap keeps widening.
Core–Periphery Model

Core = Developed countries (produce expensive manufactured goods). Periphery = Developing countries (export cheap raw materials). The core benefits far more from trade.

Declining Terms of Trade (Prebisch-Singer Thesis)

Over time, prices of raw materials FALL relative to manufactured goods. So poor countries must export MORE and MORE commodities just to buy the SAME amount of imports. They're running faster just to stay in place.

Import Substitution Industrialisation (ISI)

The proposed solution: instead of importing manufactured goods, develop your OWN domestic industries behind protective tariffs (taxes on imports). Government actively builds an industrial base to replace imports.

Weak Linkages

Primary export sectors (tea, rubber) rarely stimulate local industry, so there's no "multiplier effect" — the money earned from exports doesn't spread through the rest of the economy.

💡
Key Phrase to Memorise

"Underdevelopment is NOT a starting stage — it is the RESULT of unequal integration into global trade."

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Colonial legacy = plantation export economy (tea, rubber). Today, apparel exports rely heavily on IMPORTED textiles and machinery. Recurring foreign exchange crises = structural dependency. Sri Lanka is stuck in a "middle-income trap" — growth without deep structural transformation.

T4

Lewis Dual Sector Model

Sir W. Arthur Lewis, 1954
Core Argument Development happens when labour MOVES from the low-productivity agricultural sector to the higher-productivity industrial/modern sector. As this shift happens, industry grows, profits are reinvested, and the economy transforms.
Traditional Agricultural Sector

Low wages, surplus labour — workers here produce very little extra (marginal product is near zero). You could remove some workers and farm output would barely change.

Modern Industrial Sector

Higher wages, capital accumulation. This sector grows by attracting rural workers. As industry expands, profits are reinvested, and more workers are pulled from farming.

Surplus Labour

Workers in agriculture whose removal would NOT reduce total farm output. Their marginal product is zero or near-zero. This means they can move to industry without harming food supply.

Lewis Turning Point

The moment when surplus labour is FULLY ABSORBED into industry. After this point, wages start to rise because labour is no longer cheap and abundant. This triggers broad-based income growth for everyone.

✅
Error Fixed

The original notes said surplus labour has marginal product "= 0" in one place and "close to zero" in another — inconsistent. The accurate statement: surplus labour has a marginal product that is zero or very close to zero (near-zero). Both phrasings appear in Lewis's original work.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Post-1977 EPZs pulled rural workers (especially women) into the apparel industry — a classic Lewis transformation. BUT a large informal sector persists (surplus labour not fully absorbed). Many foreign-owned firms repatriate profits instead of reinvesting. Sri Lanka had a PARTIAL Lewis transformation.

T5

Hirschman's Unbalanced Growth Theory

Albert O. Hirschman, 1958
Core Argument You DON'T need to develop all sectors at once. Instead, FOCUS investment in a few KEY sectors that generate the most "linkages" to other industries. This creates a chain reaction of growth.
Backward Linkages (Upstream)

When an industry grows, it INCREASES DEMAND for its suppliers. Example: An apparel factory needs fabric suppliers, button makers, dye producers. Growth in apparel = growth in these supply industries.

Forward Linkages (Downstream)

When an industry grows, its OUTPUT becomes INPUT for OTHER industries further down the chain. Example: Steel production → enables cars, construction, appliances. More steel = more industries become possible.

Unbalanced Growth

Intentionally investing in strategic sectors that have HIGH LINKAGE POTENTIAL, rather than spreading investment evenly. The "imbalance" creates pressure for other sectors to grow to meet demand — like a domino effect.

✅
Error Fixed: Sri Lanka Apparel Linkages

The original notes claimed apparel has "strong forward linkages." This is misleading. Sri Lanka's apparel sector is more accurately characterised by weak backward linkages — the domestic textile industry is too small to supply the apparel factories, so Sri Lanka must import most of its fabric. The forward linkages (into shipping, logistics) exist but are not the defining feature. The weakness is in backward linkages (upstream suppliers).

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Sri Lanka's apparel sector has weak backward linkages — it must import fabric, chemicals, and machinery (no strong domestic textile industry). This explains why Sri Lanka succeeded in apparel exports but failed to diversify industrially — the linkages that would stimulate other industries are missing.

T6

Dependency Theory

Andre Gunder Frank, Samir Amin
Core Argument Underdevelopment is NOT a failure of poor countries to "catch up" — it is CAUSED by rich countries exploiting poor ones. The same global capitalism that makes core nations rich KEEPS periphery nations poor.
Unequal Exchange

Periphery exports LOW-VALUE goods (raw cotton) and imports HIGH-VALUE goods (electronics, machinery). The terms are inherently unfair — poor countries always get less value than they give.

MNC Control

Multinational Corporations control branding, technology, and marketing — capturing most of the profit. Local countries just do the low-value assembly work.

Profit Repatriation

Foreign companies take profits BACK to their home countries instead of reinvesting locally — this drains money from the developing economy.

Debt Dependence

Borrowing forces developing countries to adopt policies (austerity, open markets) that favour creditors (like the IMF), not local development needs.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Garment factories do LOW-VALUE assembly. Western brands capture the branding and design value. Sri Lanka imports textiles, machinery, and packaging. Heavy external borrowing → IMF dependency. This is a textbook dependency theory example.

💡
Critique of Dependency Theory

It ignores internal governance problems (corruption, bad policies). It also cannot explain why South Korea and Taiwan — both poor ex-colonies — managed to break out of dependency and become wealthy. The theory explains traps but not escapes.

T7

Sustainable Development

Brundtland Commission (WCED), 1987
Definition (Memorise Word for Word) "Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." — WCED, Our Common Future, 1987

Core idea: Development must balance THREE pillars simultaneously. You cannot sacrifice one for another — they must all be maintained at the same time.

Economic Sustainability

Stable, diversified growth that reduces poverty without depleting resources. The economy must keep functioning for future generations.

Social Sustainability

Equity, protection of vulnerable groups, participatory governance, basic services for all. Everyone has a fair share.

Environmental Sustainability

Conservation of ecosystems, reducing pollution, climate resilience. The planet must remain liveable for future generations.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Sri Lanka is highly climate-vulnerable — droughts, floods, and coastal erosion. Tea and agriculture sectors face yield declines from climate change. Goal: 70% renewable energy by 2030, but implementation challenges persist.

T8

Global Value Chain (GVC) Theory

Gary Gereffi et al.
Core Argument Production is now globally fragmented — different countries do different stages. A phone is designed in the USA, assembled in China, using chips from Taiwan. Countries need to UPGRADE to move into higher-value stages.
Where the Money Is in a Value Chain

HIGH VALUE: Design, R&D, branding, marketing, retail, technology (done by rich countries)
LOW VALUE: Basic assembly, raw material processing (where poor countries usually are stuck)

4 Types of Upgrading (How Countries Move Up)

Process Upgrading

Make the SAME product more efficiently — reduce waste, improve speed, lower costs.

Product Upgrading

Make a MORE ADVANCED or higher-quality product — move from basic to premium.

Functional Upgrading

Move into HIGHER-VALUE functions — branding, design, logistics, marketing. Stop just assembling and start designing.

Inter-sectoral Upgrading

Apply skills learned in one industry to a completely NEW industry — diversify using accumulated capabilities.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

MAS Holdings and Brandix = functional upgrading (into R&D and innovation). Most SMEs are stuck in CMT (Cut-Make-Trim) — basic assembly — capturing minimal value. Goal: move into design, branding, and logistics.

T9

Inclusive Growth Theory

Various — UNDP, World Bank
Core Argument Traditional growth doesn't automatically reduce poverty or inequality. Inclusive growth ensures BROAD PARTICIPATION — growth must reach women, rural populations, SMEs, youth, and marginalised groups.
🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Female labour force participation is only ~34%, reflecting cultural and structural constraints. Samurdhi/Aswesuma social protection programmes exist but need better targeting. Skills mismatch due to education–job market disconnect.

Part Four

Economic Diversification & Specialisation

Two strategies that seem like opposites but actually work best together. Smart regions use both.

Economic Diversification
  • Broadening the economic base — many sectors
  • Reduces dependence on a single product or industry
  • Increases resilience to external shocks
  • Spreads risk across multiple activities
  • Think: "Don't put all your eggs in one basket"
Economic Specialisation
  • Concentrating resources in sectors where a region excels
  • Exploits comparative and competitive advantages
  • Enables productivity gains through economies of scale
  • Builds deep expertise and industry clusters
  • Think: "Be the absolute best at one thing"
💡
Exam Trap

Don't treat these as opposites! They are TWO COMPLEMENTARY strategies. Diversification = safety net (spread risk). Specialisation = competitive power. Smart regions balance BOTH. If the exam asks "should a region diversify or specialise?" — your answer is BOTH, used strategically.

Key Terms You Must Be Able to Explain

Comparative Advantage (Ricardo, 1817)

The ability to produce a good at a LOWER OPPORTUNITY COST than a competitor. It's not about being the best — it's about what you give up. Even if Sri Lanka can produce both tea and electronics, if tea costs relatively less to produce → specialise in tea.

Opportunity Cost

What you GIVE UP when you make a choice. If a region grows tea instead of rubber, the opportunity cost is the rubber it didn't produce. Every decision has a trade-off.

Economies of Scale

As you produce MORE, the cost per unit FALLS. Bigger factories, shared infrastructure, bulk purchasing all reduce costs — making specialised regions more globally competitive.

Agglomeration Economies

Cost advantages from GEOGRAPHIC CLUSTERING. When related firms locate together, they share labour pools, suppliers, knowledge, and infrastructure — lowering overall costs. Examples: Silicon Valley (ICT), Katunayake EPZ (apparel).

Knowledge Spillovers

When firms cluster together, ideas and innovations FLOW FREELY between them — employees carry knowledge when moving between firms. The whole cluster becomes MORE productive than the sum of its parts.

Product Life Cycle

Industries go through stages: introduction → growth → maturity → decline. As an industry matures in one region, it relocates to lower-cost regions, allowing the original region to DIVERSIFY into new industries.

Part Five

Regional Development — Core Concepts

What exactly is "regional development"? How do we define a region? How do we measure it?

5.1 What is Regional Development?

OECD Definition

"A general effort to reduce regional disparities by supporting economic activities in regions." (OECD, 2019)

In simpler terms: Regional development is the economic and social progress made in specific geographic areas within a country. Its primary aim is to reduce the gap between rich and poor regions by directing resources to underdeveloped areas.

Key features: It is multi-dimensional (economic + social + environmental + institutional). It is place-based (uses each region's unique strengths). It is aligned with global frameworks like SDGs 10 and 11.

5.2 Sri Lanka's Core–Periphery Problem

Sri Lanka has a severe CORE–PERIPHERY structure. The Western Province dominates income, investment, infrastructure, and innovation. Every other province is peripheral to varying degrees.

Part Seven

Classical Location Theories

These theories answer: WHERE do economic activities locate and WHY? They explain why industries, cities, and farms cluster where they do.

L1

Von Thünen's Agricultural Location Model

Von Thünen, 1826
Core Idea Where do different types of farms locate around a market town? Answer: farmers locate based on how much profit they can make after paying transport costs. The closer to market, the less you pay to transport goods, so the more profitable you are.
Locational Rent Formula
Locational Rent = (Market Price − Production Cost) − (Transport Cost × Distance)
The farther you are from the market, the higher your transport cost, and the less profit (rent) you earn. This creates rings: perishable high-value crops closest in; extensive grain farming farther out.
Isotropic Surface

The model assumes a perfectly uniform flat plain — same soil, climate, and transport costs everywhere. This is an idealised simplification to isolate the effect of distance.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Urban fringe farming near Colombo (vegetables, dairy) follows Von Thünen's logic — intensive, high-value, perishable goods locate close to the market. Extensive rice farming is further away.

L2

Weber's Theory of Industrial Location

Alfred Weber, 1909
Core Idea Where should a factory locate? Answer: at the point that MINIMISES total transport costs for bringing raw materials IN and shipping finished goods OUT.
Weight-Losing Industries

Industries where raw materials LOSE significant weight during production (e.g., iron ore → steel: lots of slag and waste). These locate NEAR RAW MATERIALS because it's cheaper to transport the lighter finished product than haul heavy raw materials to a distant factory.

Weight-Gaining Industries

Industries where the product GAINS weight or bulk during production (e.g., bottling — adding water or liquid). These locate NEAR THE MARKET because you'd be transporting heavy finished goods if you made them elsewhere.

Agglomeration Economies (Weber)

Cost advantages from CLUSTERING with other firms. Shared workers, suppliers, and services mean firms locate together because it's cheaper than operating alone.

✅ Isodapane — Corrected Definition

Lines connecting points of equal TOTAL transport cost around a factory. They show how much extra cost a factory would incur if it moved away from the least-cost location. The critical isodapane is where labour savings exactly equal the additional transport costs — not the isodapane itself.

✅
Error Fixed: Two Corrections Here

(1) Isodapane definition: An isodapane connects points of equal total transport cost — NOT "equal additional transport costs." The word "additional" was wrong.
(2) Terminology: The correct Weber term is "weight-losing" industries. One section used "weight-saving" — that is incorrect and inconsistent.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Major industries cluster near Colombo Port — weight-losing logic (cheap to export finished goods). EPZs: Katunayake (10km from port), Biyagama (25km), Seethawaka (55km). Inland provinces like Uva face higher logistics costs — Weber explains why investment avoids them.

L3

Christaller's Central Place Theory

Walter Christaller, 1933
Core Idea Why do cities and towns form in the sizes and locations they do? Answer: settlements are "central places" that provide goods and services to surrounding areas. Their size and spacing is governed by the minimum demand needed to support services and how far people are willing to travel for them.
Threshold

The MINIMUM POPULATION (or demand) needed to support a service. A heart surgeon needs a much bigger threshold than a grocery store. Low threshold = common service everywhere. High threshold = only in big cities.

Range

The MAXIMUM DISTANCE consumers are willing to travel for a service. People travel far for a specialist hospital, but only a short distance for daily bread. Higher range = service only in large urban centres.

Hierarchy of Settlements

Higher-order services (hospitals, universities, finance) are found only in LARGE CITIES. Lower-order services (basic shops, clinics) exist in SMALLER TOWNS. This creates a nested hierarchy.

Hexagonal Market Areas

Christaller predicted that settlements arrange in a hexagonal grid pattern to maximise coverage efficiency — hexagons tile perfectly with no gaps or overlaps in service areas.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Colombo = primate city (highest-order services). Kandy, Galle, Jaffna = regional centres. District capitals serve surrounding rural regions. Mullaitivu and Monaragala LACK major service hubs → poor access to services → spatial inequality.

Part Eight

Regional Spatial Theories

These theories explain why development concentrates in some areas and not others — and what to do about it.

R1

Growth Pole Theory

François Perroux, 1955
Core Idea Development doesn't spread evenly — it starts from concentrated clusters ("poles") of economic activity. These poles generate spillovers that stimulate growth in surrounding areas.

A growth pole is a geographic concentration of economic activity (e.g., an industrial cluster, EPZ, port) that generates strong linkages, attracts investment, and stimulates broader regional development through spillover effects — benefits flowing outward to surrounding regions.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

BOI zones (Katunayake) = growth poles. Colombo and Gampaha = major growth poles. Hambantota and Trincomalee = attempted new poles, with mixed results. The Western Province pole created LIMITED benefits for inland regions — weak spillovers show the theory's limits.

R2

Myrdal's Cumulative Causation Theory

Gunnar Myrdal, 1957
Core Idea Development is SELF-REINFORCING. Rich regions get richer, poor regions get poorer — not because of individual failures, but because of built-in structural forces that create a feedback loop. Left alone, markets make inequality WORSE.
Spread Effects (Positive)

Positive spillovers FROM prosperous regions TO poorer ones: technology transfer, remittances, knowledge flows. These can help lagging regions IF connectivity is strong enough.

Backwash Effects (Negative)

NEGATIVE flows FROM poor regions TO rich ones: skilled workers migrate away, capital moves to richer regions. These DEPLETE lagging regions and make them weaker over time.

Cumulative Causation (The Vicious/Virtuous Cycle)

Rich regions attract more investment → more growth → more attractive → even more investment (virtuous cycle upward). Poor regions lose workers → less investment → less growth → fewer workers stay (vicious cycle downward).

💡
Myrdal's Policy Conclusion

Markets LEFT ALONE make inequality WORSE. Strong government intervention is REQUIRED — targeted infrastructure, investment incentives, and regional programmes — to counteract backwash effects.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Young rural workers migrate to Colombo or abroad — draining Northern, Uva, and Eastern provinces. Plantation areas chronically poor due to weak spread effects. Centralised investment model intensifies backwash effects.

R3

New Economic Geography (NEG)

Paul Krugman, 1991
Core Idea Economic activities cluster in CORES due to increasing returns, lower transport costs, and larger markets. This creates core-periphery patterns — the bigger the core, the more it attracts, and the more the periphery falls behind.
Centripetal Forces (Pull inward)

Forces that PULL economic activity toward the core: shared labour markets, shared infrastructure, knowledge spillovers, supplier density, large consumer markets. These make cores grow.

Centrifugal Forces (Push outward)

Forces that PUSH activity away from the core: congestion, high rent, pollution, distance disadvantages. These can push some industries toward the periphery.

Increasing Returns to Scale

Larger production means lower costs per unit AND more innovation from concentrated talent. This makes big cores even more attractive to new firms — a self-reinforcing advantage.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Western Province = strong centripetal forces. Northern, Uva, Eastern = centrifugal disadvantages. NEG shows regional inequality WIDENS without intervention. Sri Lanka's expressway network aims to reduce the "distance penalty" on lagging regions.

R4

Endogenous Growth Theory

Paul Romer (1986, 1990); Robert Lucas (1988)
Core Idea Growth comes from WITHIN the economy — from human capital, innovation, and knowledge. Unlike older theories that treated technology as an external gift, endogenous theory says regions can INVEST in their own growth engines.

"Endogenous" means "coming from inside." Growth is generated internally by investment in education, skills, R&D, and knowledge — not by luck or external forces.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Regions with strong universities (Colombo, Peradeniya) show higher economic dynamism. Estate sector has human capital DEFICITS — low education attainment limits growth. Policy: decentralise higher education, promote regional innovation hubs.

R5

Cluster Theory

Michael Porter, 1990
Core Idea Geographically concentrated industries gain COMPETITIVENESS through shared suppliers, skills, infrastructure, and a culture of innovation. Clusters create competitive advantage for both firms and regions.
Porter's Diamond — 4 Factors That Make Clusters Competitive

(1) Factor Conditions: Skilled labour, resources, infrastructure in the region
(2) Demand Conditions: Sophisticated local buyers who push firms to innovate
(3) Related & Supporting Industries: Nearby suppliers and complementary sectors
(4) Firm Strategy, Structure & Rivalry: Competition among local firms drives continuous improvement

✅
Error Fixed: Porter's Diamond

The original notes misnamed the 4th diamond factor as just "rivalry." The correct name is "Firm Strategy, Structure, and Rivalry" — all three elements together. Additionally, Porter identified Government and Chance as two additional modifying factors (not part of the core diamond, but important).

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

Apparel clusters (Katunayake, Biyagama, Koggala) show strong global performance. Tourism clusters (South Coast, East Coast). IT/BPO cluster in Colombo. Lagging provinces lack the supporting institutions needed for clusters to form.

R6

Decentralisation Theory

Various — North, Oates
Core Idea Transferring POWER, RESOURCES, and DECISION-MAKING from the central government to regional and local authorities enables more effective, responsive, and inclusive regional development.
Fiscal Decentralisation

Giving provincial/local governments their OWN tax revenue and budgetary authority — not just transfers from the centre. Currently WEAK in Sri Lanka.

Political Decentralisation

Elected regional governments with real power to make policy decisions. Example: Provincial Councils under Sri Lanka's 13th Amendment (1987).

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka Link

13th Amendment (1987) created Provincial Councils. But fiscal decentralisation remains weak — provinces are heavily dependent on central government transfers. Post-conflict Northern and Eastern provinces need stronger local institutions.

Part Nine

Planning & Regional Planning Techniques

Planning is about deciding in advance what needs to be done — thinking before acting, bridging where we are with where we want to go.

9.1 What is Planning?

Planning is a KEY FUNCTION OF MANAGEMENT — deciding in ADVANCE what needs to be done. It is "thinking before doing." Planning bridges the gap from WHERE WE ARE to WHERE WE WANT TO GO.

✅
Errors Fixed: Misattributed Quotes & Wrong Scholar Name

(1) "If you fail to plan, you are planning to fail" is commonly attributed to Benjamin Franklin, but there is no verified source in his writings. It should be noted as a common planning maxim or "attributed to" Franklin — not quoted as a confirmed Franklin statement.
(2) "Action without a plan leads to chaos" is not a verified Fayol quote. Henri Fayol's actual principle was that planning (prevoyance) is the first function of management — don't attribute this specific phrase to him.
(3) "Cyril L Hudson" does not appear in standard management literature. The definition attributed to him may belong to another scholar. Treat with caution in the exam.

9.2 Definitions from Scholars

Peter Drucker

"Planning is a managerial function that includes decisions to ensure future results."

Koontz and O'Donnell

"Planning is deciding in advance what to do, how to do it, when to do it, and who is to do it. Planning bridges the gap from where we are to where we want to go."

What a Plan Is

A plan is a predetermined written document of intended future course of action aimed at achieving specific objectives within a specific time, quality, and cost. It answers: WHAT to be done, WHY, WHEN, WHERE, HOW, HOW MUCH, BY WHOM, and at what COST and TIME.

Triple Constraints of a Plan: (1) Specified BUDGET · (2) Specified TIME · (3) Specified QUALITY. Any plan that ignores one of these will fail.

9.3 Planning vs. Plan

PlanningA Plan
A PROCESS — continuous thinking, analysing, decidingA DOCUMENT — a written record produced at a point in time
Ongoing and continuous — never fully finishedStatic output that captures decisions made
Aims at achieving goals within resources, quality, and timeframeFinancial resources are expended to create capital assets/services

9.4 The 8 Characteristics of Planning

1. Intellectual Process

A mental exercise — thinking ahead before acting. Requires analysis, judgment, and foresight.

2. Futuristic Function

Planning looks AHEAD — it is a predisposition for things that haven't happened yet.

3. Primary Function of Management

The FOUNDATION of all management. Policies, programmes, and procedures all stem from planning.

4. Goal-Oriented

Not random — always targets predetermined goals and objectives.

5. Continuous Process

Never-ending — plan → implement → review → replan. The environment keeps changing.

6. Flexible Process

Must be adaptable — PESTEL forces (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal) change and make the future unpredictable.

7. Problem-Solving Process

Identifies the BEST ALTERNATIVE from all possible options to solve future problems before they occur.

8. Instrument for Efficiency

Achieves objectives at MINIMUM COST. Avoids waste of the 5 Ms: Men, Money, Machines, Materials, and Management.

✅
Error Fixed: The 5 Ms

The original notes listed the 5th M as "Methods." The more widely accepted 5th M in management literature is "Management" (not Methods). Some sources use Methods, but Management is the standard version.

9.6 Location Quotient (LQ) — Full Explanation

LQ measures how SPECIALISED a region is in a particular sector compared to the national average. It answers: "Is this sector more important here than it is in the country as a whole?"

LQ Formula
LQ = (Regional employment in sector i ÷ Total regional employment) ÷ (National employment in sector i ÷ Total national employment)
This compares TWO RATIOS:
Ratio 1: How important is sector i in THIS region?
Ratio 2: How important is sector i in the WHOLE COUNTRY?

Note: LQ can also be calculated using output (GDP/GVA) instead of employment — either approach is valid.
LQ ValueWhat It MeansImplication for Planning
LQ > 1Region is MORE specialised than national averageSector is a regional strength — likely export-oriented. Build on this.
LQ = 1Region matches the national averageSector serves local demand only — not a distinctive advantage
LQ < 1Region is UNDER-REPRESENTEDRegion likely imports this good/service — possible gap or opportunity
💡
LQ Limitation

LQ tells you WHERE you are strong — not HOW GOOD or HOW PRODUCTIVE you are. A high LQ doesn't measure competitiveness directly. Always interpret LQ alongside productivity data and market analysis.

Cluster 1 — The Regional Inequality Theories

These theories all explain the same phenomenon — why development concentrates in some places and leaves others behind — but from different angles.

Myrdal's Cumulative Causation (1957)
  • Markets CAUSE inequality through backwash effects
  • Rich regions attract workers and capital from poor regions
  • Only government intervention can reverse this
New Economic Geography — Krugman (1991)
  • Formalises Myrdal with mathematics
  • Centripetal forces = same idea as backwash effects
  • Adds: transport costs and increasing returns explain clustering
Growth Pole Theory — Perroux (1955)
  • Describes the same concentration, but is more optimistic
  • Argues poles can be DESIGNED to spread development via spillovers
  • Policy tool: invest in strategic poles, not everywhere
Core-Periphery (Structural Theory / Dependency)
  • Same pattern at the GLOBAL scale: rich nations = core, poor = periphery
  • Connected to Myrdal: backwash effects operate globally too
  • Sri Lanka = periphery to Western fashion brands
🔗
Connection: Myrdal → Krugman → Growth Pole

Myrdal (1957) described the mechanism of cumulative causation informally. Krugman (1991) modelled it mathematically using transport costs and increasing returns — giving it rigour. Growth Pole Theory uses the same insight but turns it into a policy tool: if concentration is inevitable, DIRECT it strategically. All three explain Western Province dominance in Sri Lanka.

Cluster 2 — Structural Transformation Theories

These theories share a common interest: how economies shift from agriculture to industry to services.

Lewis Dual Sector Model (T4)
  • Labour moves from farm to factory → transformation
  • Surplus agricultural labour is the engine
  • Lewis Turning Point = full absorption
Structural Theory (T3)
  • Transformation blocked by unfair global trade
  • ISI proposed as the solution: build your own industry
  • Complements Lewis: external barriers to what Lewis describes internally
Hirschman's Unbalanced Growth (T5)
  • Tells you WHERE to invest during transformation
  • Choose sectors with high backward & forward linkages
  • Hirschman answers: "which factory should Sri Lanka build first?"
Rostow's Stages (T2)
  • Describes the SEQUENCE of transformation in stages
  • Rostow says what Lewis does not: there are preconditions needed before take-off
  • Both describe a shift from agriculture to industry
🔗
Connection: Lewis + Hirschman + Structural Theory in Sri Lanka

Lewis explains HOW Sri Lanka's apparel industry grew (pulling rural labour). Hirschman explains WHY it didn't diversify (weak backward linkages — no domestic textile industry). Structural Theory explains the underlying cause (global trade structure keeps Sri Lanka in low-value assembly). Together, these three theories fully explain Sri Lanka's "partial transformation" trap.

Cluster 3 — Competitiveness & Knowledge Theories

Endogenous Growth Theory (R4)
  • Growth comes from WITHIN: human capital, R&D, knowledge
  • Regions can INVEST in their own growth engines
  • Emphasises education and innovation
Cluster Theory — Porter (R5)
  • Shows WHERE endogenous growth concentrates: in clusters
  • Knowledge spillovers (endogenous theory) are what make clusters work
  • Porter's Diamond operationalises what Romer theorised
GVC Theory (T8)
  • Shows HOW countries use knowledge to upgrade within chains
  • Functional upgrading = moving into design, branding, R&D = endogenous growth in practice
  • Clusters (Porter) support upgrading (GVC)
New Economic Geography (R3)
  • Knowledge spillovers are a CENTRIPETAL FORCE (Krugman)
  • Endogenous growth provides the CONTENT of what spills over
  • Together: knowledge clusters form in cores, widening the gap with periphery

Where Theories Disagree — Critical Tensions

Understanding where theories contradict each other shows examiner-level depth of understanding.

TensionTheory ATheory BHow to Resolve It
Markets vs. Government Classical Theory: free markets drive growth best Myrdal / Structural: markets make inequality worse and need intervention Both are partly right. Markets are efficient for resource allocation but create spatial inequality. Government intervention is needed to correct market failures in lagging regions.
Internal vs. External causes of poverty Institutional Theory: bad governance is the real cause Dependency Theory: the global system is designed to keep poor countries poor Both factors operate simultaneously. South Korea escaped dependency through institutional reform AND industrial policy — showing internal governance matters even in an unfair system.
Balance vs. Imbalance Balanced growth: invest everywhere at once for structural change Hirschman's Unbalanced Growth: focus on key sectors with most linkages Hirschman's view is more practical for resource-poor developing countries. You can't invest everywhere simultaneously — pick high-linkage sectors first.
Linear vs. Non-linear development Rostow: all countries follow the same 5 stages Dependency / Structural: some countries are structurally prevented from climbing the stages Development paths are not universal. Colonial history, trade structures, and governance create very different starting conditions — Rostow's model is too simplistic.

Exam Strategy

How to turn these notes into exam marks.

How to Answer Any Theory Question

①
WHO said it

Name the scholar and year first. "According to Lewis (1954)..." This signals to the examiner you know your sources.

②
WHAT is the core argument

One clear sentence: "Lewis argues that development happens when surplus agricultural labour moves into higher-productivity industry."

③
KEY TERMS — explain each one

Don't just list terms. Define each in your own words: "'Surplus labour' refers to agricultural workers whose removal would not reduce farm output, because their marginal product is near zero."

④
CRITICISM — at least 2 critiques

Show critical thinking. E.g., "A key limitation is that Lewis assumes industrial profits will be reinvested locally, but in Sri Lanka, foreign firms repatriate profits instead."

⑤
SRI LANKA EXAMPLE

Always connect to Sri Lanka. This is the most important step for this course. Use specific examples: Western Province, apparel industry, post-1977 liberalisation, Northern Province post-conflict, estate sector poverty.

For Formula Questions

①
State the formula

Write it out clearly: g = ((Yt − Yt-1) / Yt-1) × 100

②
Define each variable

Yt = Real GDP in the current year; Yt-1 = Real GDP in the previous year

③
Explain what it measures in plain English

"This formula calculates the percentage change in output between two years, telling us how fast the economy is growing."

④
Give a numeric example

"If GDP was Rs. 10,000bn in 2022 and Rs. 10,500bn in 2023, g = (500/10,000) × 100 = 5% growth."

💡
Final Reminder

The exam is about UNDERSTANDING, not memorising data. You need to EXPLAIN the terms, not just name them. "Backwash effects" is worth nothing if you can't say what actually flows from poor to rich regions and WHY. Sri Lanka is your best friend — every theory should connect to a Sri Lanka example.
